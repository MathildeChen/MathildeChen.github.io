NEW STATISTICAL APPROACHES FOR THE INTEGRATED ANALYSIS OF MULTI-SOURCE ANDMULTI-SCALE DATA IN PLANT EPIDEMIOLOGY: APPLICATION TO CASSAVA WITCHES' BROOM DISEASE

Summary
======
This PhD research focuses on improving the surveillance of emerging crop diseases, whose frequency is increasing as a result of climate change and globalization. These diseases pose a major threat to agricultural production and food security, making it necessary to develop systems capable of rapidly detecting disease emergence, assessing epidemic risk, and optimizing surveillance strategies. The work aims to address three main methodological challenges: 

(1) integrating multi-source data (e.g., environmental, agricultural, spatial, and societal),which are often heterogeneous, high-dimensional, and correlated, into epidemiological models; 

(2) developing approaches able to analyze epidemic risk consistently across multiple spatial scales, from the plant or field level to the regional scale, in order to effectively guide surveillance efforts; and 

(3) simultaneously optimizing multiple objectives (e.g., minimizing risk and costs) in the design of surveillance and risk management scenarios.

To this end, the thesis proposes the development of spatio-temporal epidemiological models within a Bayesian framework, allowing the integration of highly diverse data while explicitly quantifying uncertainty. Variable selection methods will be used to identify the most influential factors driving disease spread. The models will then be used to design surveillance strategies tailored to risk levels, while accounting for trade-offs between effectiveness, cost, and feasibility.
The methodological tools will first be tested on a detailed dataset to establish an analysis protocol and assess sensitivity to missing or degraded data. This protocol will then be applied to cassava witches’ broom disease, recently detected in French Guiana, which poses a serious threat to local food security. The ultimate objective is to develop tools to optimize surveillance and risk management in collaboration with field stakeholders, and more broadly to propose a methodological framework transferable to other plant, animal, or human diseases.


Research context and research question
======

Climate change and the intensification of international trade driven by globalization are fostering the emergence of new crop diseases, posing a growing threat to agricultural production and food security (Ristaino et al., 2021). To mitigate and anticipate these threats, it is essential to detect emerging outbreaks at an early stage, assess epidemic risk, and identify the optimal locations and periods for disease monitoring through the implementation of effective epidemiological surveillance systems (Parnell et al., 2017). However, the design of such systems raises several methodological challenges.

The first challenge concerns the integration of environmental, agricultural, spatial, and socio-economic data into the epidemiological predictions that underpin surveillance systems. In addition to varying across space and time, these datasets are typically heterogeneous, high-dimensional, and often redundant, making their incorporation into current surveillance frameworks particularly challenging. Traditional epidemiological approaches—such as generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs), susceptible–infectious–removed (SIR) models, and mechanistic models—still make only limited use of this diversity of information and generally treat different sources of variability separately. Yet, integrating heterogeneous data represents a major opportunity to improve disease surveillance (Robin et al., 2019) and to enhance our understanding of epidemiological processes (e.g., Motisi et al., 2022; Oro et al., 2012; Leandro-Muñoz et al., 2017). This issue is especially critical for emerging diseases, for which limited knowledge is available regarding their biological cycle or epidemic dynamics. In such contexts, integrating multiple sources of information can substantially improve the analysis of surveillance systems operating under constrained or incomplete monitoring conditions.

The second challenge is to develop models that are sufficiently flexible to provide meaningful analyses across multiple spatial scales. Although epidemics unfold at the scale of individual plants, fields, production areas, or entire regions, the literature suggests that surveillance must be implemented at broader spatial scales to achieve optimal effectiveness (Radici et al., 2023; Soubeyrand et al., 2024). However, the way in which epidemiological dynamics observed at these different scales should be integrated and articulated within a unified surveillance framework remains poorly understood.
Beyond estimating epidemic risk, evaluating the feasibility of a surveillance strategy also requires consideration of additional criteria, such as implementation costs (e.g., Parnell et al., 2017; Mastin et al., 2020) and its acceptability to local stakeholders involved in surveillance activities (e.g., Bagavathiananan et al., 2019). Consequently, surveillance strategies must be tailored to the level of epidemic risk while balancing multiple objectives to maximize the overall effectiveness of the surveillance system.


By addressing these methodological challenges, this PhD project in applied statistics aims to develop Bayesian spatio-temporal epidemiological models that integrate heterogeneous, multi-source, and multi-scale data within an epidemiological surveillance framework. A Bayesian approach is particularly well suited to this problem because it naturally accommodates diverse data types while explicitly quantifying the different sources of uncertainty. In addition, variable selection methods will be employed to identify the most influential predictors among a large set of candidate variables (Chen et al., 2023; Denis and Tadesse, 2023). The proposed models will first be evaluated using a “complete” dataset, generated from a balanced monitoring scheme including disease observations and measurements of heterogeneous explanatory variables at high spatial and/or temporal resolution. This first stage will enable the development of an analysis protocol for identifying the main sources of variability driving disease spread. The resulting protocol will subsequently be applied to a range of case studies involving plant, animal, or human epidemics.


The case study selected for this PhD project is Cassava Witches’ Broom Disease (CWBD). This disease, caused by the fungal pathogen Rhizoctonia (syn. Ceratobasidium) theobromae (Leiva et al., 2023), was detected in 2023 in French Guiana (Pardo et al., 2024) and in the Brazilian state of Amapá (ref). Owing to its severe impact on local cassava production, CWBD poses a significant threat to the food security of Indigenous communities, which rely heavily on cassava as both a staple food and an important source of income. Ultimately, the objective of this PhD project is to apply the developed models to data collected by FREDON French Guiana and to co-develop decision-support tools and risk management scenarios to enhance disease surveillance in French Guiana.
To achieve this objective, the PhD project is structured around the following research areas.


Methodological developments: 
======

1)	Identify and develop innovative, flexible Bayesian spatio-temporal epidemiological models capable of integrating heterogeneous factors that vary over time and/or space, accommodating multiple levels of analysis (from the individual plant to the regional scale), and capturing complex epidemiological processes (Ingebrigtsen et al., 2014; Nothdurft, 2020).

2)	Develop indices characterizing factors such as neighborhood structure, microclimate, varietal mixtures, and related variables to address redundancy and reduce data dimensionality (Allinne et al., 2026).

3)	Derive risk-adapted surveillance strategies from these models and investigate approaches for incorporating risk–cost trade-offs, for example through the use of multi-objective optimization models (Parnell et al., 2017; Mastin et al., 2020; Rimbaud et al., 2019).
Implementation of the developed methods

4)	Apply the methodology to a complete dataset in order to establish an analysis protocol. This will include assessing the impact of data degradation (e.g., reduced temporal resolution, heterogeneous spatial sampling, degraded external covariates) and identifying optimal model settings through sensitivity analyses (Rimbaud et al., 2018).

5)	Apply the resulting protocol to the cassava dataset, which is less complete owing to less frequent monitoring, missing observations, and an unbalanced sampling design.

Consequently, surveillance strategies must be tailored to the level of epidemic risk while balancing these different criteria to maximize the overall effectiveness of the surveillance system.

The expected outcomes of the project include:

-	The development of Bayesian spatio-temporal models integrating multi-source and multi-scale data, with applications to plant, animal, and human epidemiology.

-	The development of a data analysis protocol for optimizing epidemiological surveillance strategies.

-	The production of decision-support tools and risk management scenarios for Cassava Witches' Broom Disease in collaboration with local stakeholders.

-	The publication of scientific articles in international peer-reviewed journals.

-	Participation in one or two international scientific conferences.

Scientific, material, and financial resources
======
The first eighteen months of the PhD will not depend on the acquisition of new data. This phase will primarily focus on the development of statistical models and the establishment of an analysis protocol using an existing, well-balanced dataset collected from experimental trials, including detailed disease monitoring and measurements of heterogeneous explanatory variables.
The second phase of the project will apply the developed protocol to a cassava disease surveillance dataset, which is less comprehensive owing to less frequent monitoring, missing observations, and an unbalanced sampling design. These data are currently being collected through the Interreg CASSAMAZONIA project (2026–2029), which has been approved, although its funding is still under negotiation, and through a complementary project submitted to the CFBBA call for proposals, which is currently under evaluation. The approval of the Interreg project ensures that these data will be available during the second year of the PhD.
The Interreg CASSAMAZONIA project also provides opportunities to support the PhD candidate through funding for:
-	participation in international scientific conferences;
-	specialized training in statistics and/or epidemiology;
-	the purchase of computing equipment (laptop);
-	the recruitment of two Master's interns to contribute to the research activities.


International collaboration
======
This PhD project is embedded within an international collaboration involving French research institutions (IRD and CNRS), institutions in French Guiana (Université de Guyane), and Brazilian partners (Embrapa and CEPLAC) through the Interreg CASSAMAZONIA project.
The project will provide the PhD candidate with valuable opportunities to collaborate with a wide range of stakeholders, including agronomists, geneticists, statistical modelers, farmers, and plant health surveillance services. The candidate will participate in the workshops and training activities organized within the project and is expected to attend at least one international scientific conference during the course of the PhD.


Research dissemination and knowledge transfer
======
The research conducted during the PhD will be disseminated through several complementary channels, including scientific publications, conference presentations, and technical reports, while complying with applicable policies on confidentiality and intellectual property.
Expected outputs include:
-	Preparation and submission of scientific articles, including: a methodological paper describing the Bayesian spatio-temporal models developed during the project; an epidemiological paper demonstrating the application of the proposed analytical framework to the development of risk management scenarios for Cassava Witches' Broom Disease in French Guiana.
-	Presentation of research findings through conference papers and posters at national and international scientific meetings.
-	Where appropriate, preparation of technical reports and decision-support documents for plant health surveillance stakeholders (e.g., FREDON and CPDH).

Planned collaborations
======
The project will primarily be carried out through collaborations between the UMR AGAP and UMR PHIM research units. Additional interactions are planned with the BioSP team in Avignon, which has internationally recognized expertise in plant epidemiological modelling, particularly for emerging plant diseases (e.g., Xylella fastidiosa).
At the international level, collaborations on statistical modelling are envisaged with researchers specializing in the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) framework, including members of the R-INLA team and researchers at King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST). Collaborations are also planned with the Department of Statistics at the Federal University of Paraná (Brazil).
Regarding epidemiological surveillance, close collaboration will be established with FREDON French Guiana and, more broadly, with stakeholders involved in the cassava production sector in French Guiana. In Brazil, partnerships are also planned with EMBRAPA Cassava & Fruits, the leading Brazilian research center for cassava, and CEPLAC (the Executive Commission for the Cocoa Crop Plan), which conducts research on cocoa and associated plant diseases.
These collaborations will be facilitated through the Interreg CASSAMAZONIA project, of which these institutions are active partners.
